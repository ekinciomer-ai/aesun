World trade has always squeezed through a handful of narrow gates. One empire tried to hold five of them at once.
The Bosporus. The mouth of the Red Sea. Hormuz. Suez. And the land bridge between the Don and the Volga.
This is the story of the Ottoman plan to lock the world's sea lanes. Some of it worked... some of it stayed a dream.
Lock number one: the Bosporus. The year is 1452.
The young Sultan Mehmed builds a fortress at the strait's narrowest point, facing an older castle on the Asian shore.
According to the sources, it took just one hundred and thirty-nine days. Its nickname said it all: the Throat Cutter.
From now on, every ship coming from the Black Sea had to stop and ask permission.
A Venetian ship that refused was sunk by cannon fire. The message was clear: we own this door now.
A year later, Constantinople fell. The first lock clicked into place.
Lock number two: the Red Sea. In 1517, Sultan Selim conquers Egypt.
Now the spice road from India runs through Ottoman land. But there's a rival: Portugal.
The Portuguese had sailed around Africa into the Indian Ocean, and they wanted to cut the spice trade off at sea.
So the Ottomans built a shipyard at Suez. They took Yemen and Aden. In 1555, they set up a province on the African shore.
Both sides of the Red Sea were now Ottoman. Lock two was done.
Lock number three: Hormuz. The gate of the Persian Gulf. And the Portuguese held it.
In 1552, the great admiral and mapmaker Piri Reis sailed from Suez with a fleet.
He captured Muscat. Then he laid siege to the fortress of Hormuz. It didn't fall.
Piri Reis turned back. In Cairo, he was tried and executed. He was over eighty years old.
The Hormuz lock never opened. And today, it's still the most talked-about waterway on Earth.
Lock number four: Suez. This time the plan was huge: connect the Mediterranean and the Red Sea with a canal.
In the late 1560s, Grand Vizier Sokollu Mehmed Pasha had the canal seriously planned.
With it, the Ottoman navy could sail straight from Istanbul into the Indian Ocean.
But wars and costs shelved the project. The Suez Canal finally opened three centuries later, in 1869.
Lock number five: the Don and the Volga. In 1569, Sokollu chased an even bigger dream.
At one point, the two great rivers come within about sixty kilometers of each other. Dig a canal, and the Black Sea meets the Caspian.
That would open a route behind Persia and into Central Asia. Diggers were sent to the steppe.
But winter came early, the Crimean Khan dragged his feet, and the Russians were strong on the Caspian. The campaign failed.
The canal joining the Don and the Volga was only built in 1952, by the Soviet Union.
So what's the score? Two locks held, one was tried by the sword, and two stayed on paper.
But look at the map: Hormuz, Suez, the Red Sea... four hundred years later, the world is still fighting over the same gates.
