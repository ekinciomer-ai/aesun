Millions of people have stared at these paintings. Most of them missed the most important detail.
Today: seven famous paintings, seven hidden details. The last one is the strangest.
Number one: The Ambassadors. Hans Holbein, 1533. The National Gallery, London.
Two wealthy French diplomats, surrounded by globes, books and scientific instruments. A show of power and knowledge.
But on the floor there's a weird, stretched-out smear. From the front, it looks like nothing.
Walk to the right side of the painting, look from the edge... and the smear becomes a skull!
The message: however powerful you are, death is always in the room. And look closer: one lute string is broken.
Number two: Landscape with the Fall of Icarus. For centuries, credited to Bruegel.
In the myth, Icarus flies too close to the sun, his wax wings melt, and he falls into the sea.
So where is Icarus? Bottom right. Two tiny legs sticking out of the water!
The farmer keeps ploughing. The shepherd looks at the sky. The ship sails on. Nobody notices.
As a Flemish proverb puts it: no plough stops for a dying man.
Number three: Napoleon Crossing the Alps. Jacques-Louis David, 1801.
A rearing horse, a flying cloak, a storm. The ultimate hero picture.
In reality, Napoleon crossed the Alps on a calm day... riding a mule.
He ordered the painting but refused to sit for it. He reportedly said it's the spirit that matters, not the likeness.
Fifty years later, Paul Delaroche painted the truth: a cold Napoleon on a tired mule.
And check the rocks in David's version: the names Bonaparte, Hannibal and Charlemagne carved in stone.
Number four: The Swing. Jean-Honoré Fragonard, 1767. The Wallace Collection, London.
A young woman in pink on a swing, one shoe flying through the air.
The young man hiding in the bushes is her lover. The older man in the back? Her clueless husband.
The baron who ordered it actually wanted a bishop pushing the swing. The first painter he asked said no.
Number five: A Bar at the Folies-Bergère. Édouard Manet, 1882.
The barmaid looks straight at us. The mirror behind her shows a packed hall.
But the reflection is wrong! Her mirror image has slid to the right, and the man she's talking to isn't in front of her at all.
And look at the top left corner: the green shoes of an acrobat on a trapeze.
Number six: A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte. Georges Seurat. The Art Institute of Chicago.
Made from millions of tiny dots of color, over two years.
What's on the elegant lady's leash on the right? A monkey!
In the French slang of the time, the word for a female monkey was a wink at women of questionable reputation. Seurat's joke is that subtle.
Number seven: Vertumnus. Giuseppe Arcimboldo.
This is a portrait of an emperor: Rudolf the Second, Holy Roman Emperor.
But his face is made entirely of fruit and vegetables! A pear for a nose, apples for cheeks, wheat for eyebrows.
Rudolf wasn't offended. He loved it. Being shown as the god of abundance suited him just fine.
